3 Intro

A system of differential equations consists of multiple differential equations that involve the same set of variables and their derivatives. These systems are commonly used to model situations where multiple related quantities change with respect to one another, typically over time. The variables can represent different physical quantities, like position, velocity, temperature, or chemical concentrations, depending on the context of the problem.

There are two main types of systems of differential equations:

  1. Ordinary Differential Equations (ODEs): These involve derivatives with respect to a single variable, usually time. Each equation in the system can include derivatives of one or more dependent variables with respect to the same independent variable. For example, a system describing the motion of a pendulum might include equations for both the angular position and angular velocity, each depending on time.

  2. Partial Differential Equations (PDEs): These involve partial derivatives, where the functions depend on several independent variables. PDE systems are common in fields like physics and engineering, especially for describing phenomena such as heat distribution or fluid flow, where changes occur across different spatial dimensions and time.

Systems of differential equations can be linear or nonlinear, and they can be solved using various methods, depending on their complexity and the characteristics of the equations involved. Analytical solutions are available for simpler systems, while numerical methods might be necessary for more complex or nonlinear systems.

3.1 Wronskian to test if vectors solutions are independent

NoteDefinition

place each vector solution in each column, then calculate the determinant of the matrix

\[\det(W)=\begin{vmatrix} a & b & c \\ d & e & f \\ g & h & i \\ \end{vmatrix} \]

if \(\det(W)\neq 0\), then solutions are independent of each other

TipExample

  2. Vector Solution \(\mathbf{y}_2(t)\): \[ \mathbf{y}_2(t) = \begin{bmatrix} t e^t \\ 1 \\ \cos(t) \end{bmatrix} \]

  3. Vector Solution \(\mathbf{y}_3(t)\): \[ \mathbf{y}_3(t) = \begin{bmatrix} e^{-t} \\ t \\ t \sin(t) \end{bmatrix} \]

4 Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors1 Method for a system of diffeq

Let’s consider a:

Note\(2\times2\) system of equations

\[\frac{dx}{dt}=ax+by\]

\[\frac{dy}{dt}=cx+dy\]

The characteristic eq\(^n\) is given by:

\[\begin{vmatrix} a-\lambda & b\\ c & d-\lambda \end{vmatrix} =\lambda^2-(a+d)\lambda+(ad-bc)=0\]

The solutions (eigenvalues) of the charact. eq\(^n\) can be either:

  1. Real distinct
  2. Real repeated
  3. Complex

\[\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}k_1\\k_2\end{bmatrix}e^{\lambda t}\] \[\begin{bmatrix} a-\lambda & b\\ c & d-\lambda \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}k_1\\k_2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix}\]

We find \(\lambda\)’s then we define the corresponding eigen vectors.

4.1 Example 1:

TipExample

\[\frac{dx}{dt}=x-y\]

\[\frac{dy}{dt}=2x+4y\]

CautionSolution

4.1.1 Step 1:(eigenvalues)

\[\begin{vmatrix} a-\lambda & b\\ c & d-\lambda \end{vmatrix}\Rightarrow\begin{vmatrix} 1-\lambda & -1\\ 2 & 4-\lambda \end{vmatrix} =0\]

\[\lambda^2-5\lambda+6=0\]

\[(\lambda-2)(\lambda-3)=0\] \[\lambda_{1,2}= \{2,3\}\]

4.1.2 Step 2:(eigenvectors)

\(\boxed{\lambda=2}\)

\[\begin{bmatrix} 1-2 & -1\\ 2 & 4-2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}k_1\\k_2\end{bmatrix}=0\]

\[\Rightarrow\begin{bmatrix} -1 & -1\\ 2& 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}k_1\\k_2\end{bmatrix}=0\]

\[-k_1-k_2=0\]

pick \(k_1=1\) \(\Rightarrow k_2=-1\) (\(k_1\) arbitrary)

Eigenvector: \(\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\end{bmatrix}\)

\(\boxed{\lambda=3}\)

\[\begin{bmatrix} 1-3 & -1\\ 2 & 4-3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}k_1\\k_2\end{bmatrix}=0\]

\[\Rightarrow\begin{bmatrix} -2 & -1\\ 2& 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}k_1\\k_2\end{bmatrix}=0\]

\[\Rightarrow 2k_1+k_2=0\Rightarrow k_1=1\Rightarrow k_2=-2\] Eigenvector: \(\begin{bmatrix}1\\-2\end{bmatrix}\)

4.1.3 Step 3:(general solution)

\[\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\end{bmatrix}c_1e^{2t}+\begin{bmatrix}1\\-2\end{bmatrix}c_2e^{3t}\]

OR

\[x(t)=c_1e^{2t}+c_2e^{3t}\] \[y(t)=-c_1e^{2t}-2c_2e^{3t}\]

4.2 Example 2

TipExample

\[\frac{dx}{dt}=3x\]

\[\frac{dy}{dt}=2x-y\]

CautionSolution
  1. Step 1(eigenvalues):

\[\begin{vmatrix} 3-\lambda & 0\\ 2 & -1-\lambda \end{vmatrix}=0\]

\[(3-\lambda)(-1-\lambda)-0=0\] \[(\lambda-3)(\lambda+1)=0\] \[\boxed{\lambda=3},\quad \boxed{\lambda=-1}\]

  1. Step 2(eigenvector 1):

\(\boxed{\lambda=3}\)

\[\begin{bmatrix} 3-3 & 0\\ 2 & -1-3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}k_1\\k_2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix}\]

\[2k_1-4k_2=0\] \[k_1-2k_2=0\] \[k_1=2k_2\]

Eigenvector 1: \(\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}\)

Step 2(eigenvector 2):

\(\boxed{\lambda=-1}\)

\[\begin{bmatrix} 3-^-1 & 0\\ 2 & -1-^-1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}k_1\\k_2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix}\]

\[4k_1+0=0\rightarrow k_1=0\rightarrow k_2=\text{my choice}=1\]

Eigenvector 2: \(\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}\)

  1. Step 3(general solution):

\[\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}c_1e^{3t}+\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}c_2e^{-t}\]

4.4 Example 3

TipExample

Find the general solution of

\[{\bf y}'=\left[\begin{array}{rrr}3&-1&-1\\-2& 3& 2\\4&-1&-2\end{array}\right]{\bf y}\]

CautionSolution

\[\left|\begin{array}{ccc}3-\lambda&-1&-1\\-2&3-\lambda& 2\\4 &-1&-2-\lambda\end{array}\right|=-(\lambda-2)(\lambda-3)(\lambda+1).\nonumber\]

CautionNarrative:

Solving a \(3 \times 3\) system of linear differential equations often involves a methodical approach that can be extended from methods used in smaller systems. Here’s a step-by-step explanation of the typical methods used, primarily focusing on homogeneous systems:

4.4.1 1. Write the System in Matrix Form

First, express the system of differential equations in matrix form. Suppose we have: \[\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = A \mathbf{x}\] where \(\mathbf{x}\) is the vector of variables, and \(A\) is a \(3 \times 3\) matrix containing the coefficients of the system.

4.4.2 2. Find the Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

The next step is to solve the characteristic equation of the matrix \(A\), which is given by: \[\det(A - \lambda I) = 0\] where \(\lambda\) is a scalar (the eigenvalue), and \(I\) is the \(3 \times 3\) identity matrix. This will yield a cubic equation in \(\lambda\), solving which gives us the eigenvalues.

4.4.3 3. Compute the Eigenvectors

For each eigenvalue \(\lambda_i\), find the corresponding eigenvector \(\mathbf{v}_i\) by solving: \[(A - \lambda_i I) \mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{0}\] This system of equations may sometimes need to be solved using methods like Gaussian elimination.

4.4.4 4. Form the General Solution

If the eigenvalues are distinct, the general solution to the differential equation can be expressed as: \[\mathbf{x}(t) = c_1 e^{\lambda_1 t} \mathbf{v}_1 + c_2 e^{\lambda_2 t} \mathbf{v}_2 + c_3 e^{\lambda_3 t} \mathbf{v}_3\] where \(c_1, c_2,\) and \(c_3\) are constants determined by the initial conditions.

4.4.5 5. Handle Special Cases

  • Repeated Eigenvalues: If any eigenvalue is repeated, you may need to find generalized eigenvectors to form a complete set of solutions.
  • Complex Eigenvalues: If the eigenvalues are complex, the solution will involve trigonometric functions (sines and cosines) and possibly exponential functions, reflecting oscillatory behavior.

4.4.6 6. Use Initial Conditions

Finally, use the initial conditions provided to solve for the constants \(c_1, c_2,\) and \(c_3\).

This approach will allow you to solve any \(3 \times 3\) system of linear differential equations, assuming it can be described by constant coefficients and is homogeneous. Non-homogeneous systems (i.e., systems with a non-zero right-hand side) would typically require additional steps such as finding a particular solution.
